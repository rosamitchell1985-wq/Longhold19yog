Breathwork basics

Five foundational breathing patterns used in yoga, with counts, setup, how long to practice, and clear notes on when to stop. Written as movement education, with no claims about what breathing does for anything other than your attention.

Read this first

Breathing practices change how you feel, and some people find that unsettling rather than pleasant. This page describes technique only. It makes no claim that any pattern here treats, prevents or improves any health condition.

Stop immediately if you feel dizzy, lightheaded, short of breath, tingly in the hands or face, anxious, or if your vision changes. Return to ordinary unmanaged breathing and stay sitting until it passes. Speak with a qualified healthcare professional before practicing breathing techniques if you are pregnant, have a respiratory or cardiovascular condition, high or low blood pressure, a history of fainting, a history of panic, or a seizure disorder. Full health and safety disclaimer.

The setup, which applies to all five

Every pattern below assumes the same starting position, and getting it right removes most of the difficulty.

  • Sit so that your spine can be upright without effort. On a chair with both feet flat is completely fine and is often better than the floor. If you sit cross-legged, sit on the front edge of a folded blanket so your hips are higher than your knees.
  • Let the ribs be free. Slumping compresses the front of the ribcage and makes every pattern harder. Sitting rigidly upright does the same from the other direction.
  • Breathe in and out through the nose, unless a pattern says otherwise.
  • Set a timer so you are not checking the clock. Two to five minutes is plenty for any pattern here.
  • Count at your own speed. A count is a unit, not a second. Find a pace where four counts is a comfortable inhale for you and keep that pace consistent.

The slim bar under the site header counts these patterns. It is currently set to box breathing, four counts in each phase. Press Start pacing and follow it. If you have reduced motion enabled in your operating system, the bar shows the pattern as text and does not animate.

1. Even breath

Sanskrit
Sama Vṛtti, meaning equal fluctuation
Count
Inhale 4, exhale 4
Duration
2 to 5 minutes, or throughout a standing pose
Where it is used
Standing poses, balance work, the whole morning sequence

The default. Inhale for four counts, exhale for four counts, through the nose, with no pause at either end. That is the entire technique.

Its usefulness is that it gives you something specific to notice while you are holding a shape, and it makes the moment your breath becomes uneven obvious. In a standing pose, the count getting shorter is the earliest signal that you have gone further than is useful today, and it arrives well before anything hurts.

If four counts feels rushed, use three. If it feels long, use five. Consistency between the two halves matters, the specific number does not.

2. Extended exhale

Sanskrit
Viṣama Vṛtti, meaning unequal fluctuation
Count
Inhale 4, exhale 6 or 8
Duration
3 to 10 minutes
Where it is used
Supported poses, the evening sequence, final rest

Inhale for four counts, exhale for six. When six is easy, try eight. There is no hold at either end.

Almost everyone finds this harder than it sounds on the first few attempts, because a long exhale needs to be paced from the start rather than dumped in the first two counts. The usual error is exhaling most of the air quickly and then squeezing out the remainder, which produces a strained final third. Let the air leave slowly and evenly all the way through.

Practice it lying down before you use it sitting up. It is easier to pace when you are not also holding yourself upright.

3. Box breathing

Also called
Square breathing, four-part breath
Count
Inhale 4, hold 4, exhale 4, hold 4
Duration
2 to 4 minutes to begin with
Where it is used
Seated practice, before a long hold, as a standalone practice

Four counts in, four counts holding with the lungs full, four counts out, four counts holding with the lungs empty. Then repeat.

The two pauses are what make it different from the first two patterns, and they are also what makes it worth being careful with. Holding the breath is a real physiological event. Start with two minutes, not ten, and shorten the holds to two counts if four feels like a strain. If you feel any urgency to breathe during a hold, the pattern is too long for you today, which is information rather than a problem.

Do not practice this while driving, in water, or standing on one leg.

4. Ocean breath

Sanskrit
Ujjāyī, often translated as victorious breath
Count
Even, 4 in and 4 out, with a slight constriction at the throat
Duration
Throughout a moving practice, or 3 to 5 minutes seated
Where it is used
Vinyasa and Ashtanga classes, moving practice generally

This is the audible breath you hear in a flowing class. The throat narrows slightly so the air makes a soft sound on the way past, a little like a distant wave or like fogging a window with your mouth closed.

To find it: open your mouth and exhale as though fogging a mirror. Notice where that happens at the back of the throat. Then close your mouth and make the same shape while breathing out through the nose. Once you have the exhale, apply the same slight narrowing to the inhale.

It should be quiet enough that only you can hear it clearly. If it is loud enough to be heard across a room, it is being forced. It should never involve tension in the jaw, the tongue or the face.

Its practical use in a moving class is timing: an audible breath makes it obvious when you are moving faster than you are breathing.

5. Alternate nostril breathing

Sanskrit
Nāḍī Śodhana
Count
Inhale 4 one side, exhale 4 the other, alternating
Duration
2 to 5 minutes
Where it is used
Seated practice, usually at the start or end of a session

Sit upright. Rest your left hand anywhere comfortable. With your right hand, bring the index and middle fingers to rest at the space between your eyebrows, leaving the thumb free on one side and the ring finger free on the other.

  • Close the right nostril with the thumb. Inhale through the left for four counts.
  • Close the left nostril with the ring finger, release the thumb. Exhale through the right for four counts.
  • Inhale through the right for four counts.
  • Close the right, release the left. Exhale through the left for four counts.
  • That is one full round. Continue for two to five minutes, and finish having exhaled through the left.

Touch the nose lightly. The fingers are a valve, not a clamp, and pressing hard is the most common mistake. Skip this pattern entirely if you have a cold, blocked sinuses or a deviated septum, because a blocked side turns it into a struggle.

How to build a breathing practice

Three to five minutes daily, at the same point in your day, produces more than twenty minutes once a week. A practical order for the first month:

  • Week one and two: even breath, three minutes, sitting on a chair. Nothing else.
  • Week three: extended exhale, four minutes, lying down with the knees supported.
  • Week four: add box breathing, two minutes only, seated, after the extended exhale.

Ocean breath belongs with movement rather than as a standalone practice, so bring it into the morning sequence once the pattern is familiar. Alternate nostril breathing is worth adding after the first month, not before.

What this page does not claim

You will find a great many claims made for breathing techniques. This site does not make them. What can be said plainly is this: these patterns give you something specific and repeatable to pay attention to, they make it obvious when your breath has become uneven during a physical pose, and many people find them a settling way to start or finish a practice.

That is the whole of it. If you are interested in the physiology, the place to take that question is a qualified healthcare professional, not a yoga website.
