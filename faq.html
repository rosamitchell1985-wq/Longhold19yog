Starting out

No. Flexibility is one possible result of practicing, not a requirement for beginning, and how far you can reach in a given pose is determined largely by skeletal proportions you did not choose. Two people with identical muscle length can be eight inches apart in a forward fold, permanently.

Every pose guide here includes at least two prop-supported options, written as equal alternatives rather than as fallbacks, so the shape fits the range of motion you have on the day you practice.

Nothing. A firm floor, clothes you can move in and a sturdy chair will carry you through your first month.

If you later want one item, buy a mat, and buy a 72-inch one if you are taller than about 5 feet 10 inches. After that: two blocks, then a strap, then a firm blanket. A hardback book substitutes for a block, a bathrobe belt for a strap and a folded bath towel for a blanket. The props guide lists a household substitute for every item.

Ten to twenty minutes, three or four days a week, is a workable first month for most adults. Frequency matters more than duration at the start, because most of what changes early is coordination rather than tissue, and coordination responds to repetition.

The beginner's guide lays out a four-week schedule using the free sequences.

Anything you can bend, kneel and lie down in that will not slide up over your head when you fold forward. Fitted at the ankle and the waist is more practical than fitted everywhere. Practice barefoot if you can; if not, socks with rubber grip on the sole are widely available. Take off watches, belts and anything that will press into you when you lie on it.

Practicing

Sensation is broad, spread over the belly of a muscle, and eases as you keep breathing. Effort is muscular work: your leg shakes, your breath wants to get shorter. Pain is sharp, hot, electrical or pinching, is usually located in a joint rather than a muscle, and does not ease as you breathe.

Come out of anything that hurts. That is what experienced practitioners do, and it is the only reliable way to keep practicing for decades.

Because almost every human body has side-to-side differences arising from handedness, from how you sit, from old injuries and from occupation. Noticing it usually happens in the second week of practicing and it is a good sign, because it means your attention has moved from getting the shape right to noticing what is happening.

Do both sides for the same length of time, note the difference, and carry on. Forcing the stiffer side to match the freer one is how people acquire the injury they were trying to avoid.

No. Wobbling is the ankle stabilizers doing their job. It is the training, not a sign of failing at the training. It also varies considerably day to day with tiredness, with the surface you are standing on and with what you did the day before.

Practice balance poses beside a wall with two fingertips touching it. That removes the fear of falling, which is often the thing actually making balance difficult. The Tree Pose guide covers this in detail.
