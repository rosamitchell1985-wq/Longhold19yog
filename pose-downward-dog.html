Downward-Facing Dog

Adho Mukha Śvānāsana  (AH-doh MOO-kah shvah-NAH-suh-nuh)

Probably the most recognized pose in modern yoga, and one of the most frequently rushed. It is an inverted V: hands and feet on the floor, hips high, spine long. It appears dozens of times in a flowing class, which makes getting the setup right worth a few minutes of reading.

Pose breakdown

Adho Mukha Śvānāsana

  • Beginner
  • Shoulders
  • Hamstrings
  • No prop required
Difficulty
Beginner. Wrist load makes it worth learning the wall version first.
Breath pattern
Even breath. Inhale 4 counts, exhale 4 counts, through the nose.
Suggested hold
5 to 8 breaths, roughly 30 to 45 seconds
What moves
Shoulder flexion, spinal length, ankle and calf range, hamstring length
Props that help
Two blocks, a wall, a chair, a folded blanket under the heels
Where it appears
10-Minute Morning, Gentle Full Practice
Entry steps
  • Start on hands and knees with your hands under your shoulders and your knees under your hips.
  • Spread your fingers wide and turn your middle fingers to point straight forward, toward the top of the mat.
  • Press the whole hand down, especially the pad at the base of the index finger. Most wrist discomfort in this pose comes from weight collecting in the heel of the hand.
  • Curl your toes under so the balls of your feet meet the mat.
  • Press the floor away and lift your knees, keeping them generously bent for now.
  • Send your hips back and up until your spine feels long from tailbone to the crown of your head.
  • Only then begin to straighten your knees, and only as far as your spine stays long. Stop the moment your lower back starts to round.
Alignment cues
  • Length before straightness. A long spine with bent knees is the pose. A straight-legged version with a rounded back is a different, less useful shape.
  • Heels do not need to touch. Where your heels land is a matter of proportions and calf length, not achievement. Many people never put them down and that is unremarkable.
  • Turn the upper arms outward. Imagine spiraling the inner elbows toward each other. This makes room at the top of the shoulder instead of jamming it.
  • Ears in line with the upper arms. Let the head hang between the arms rather than lifting it to look forward, which compresses the back of the neck.
  • Ribs in. If the lowest ribs are pushing toward the floor, draw them gently back so the length lives in the spine, not in the front of the ribcage.
  • Weight spread front to back. Aim for roughly even pressure between hands and feet rather than all of it hanging in the arms.
Common mistakes
  • Straightening the legs first. This is the single most common one. It pulls the pelvis under, rounds the lower back and turns a spine pose into a hamstring struggle.
  • Hands too far forward. The pose gets longer and flatter, the wrists take more load, and the shoulders end up carrying the whole thing.
  • Gripping the fingers. Clawing the mat is a sign the weight is sitting in the wrist heel. Spread the fingers and press the pads down instead.
  • Holding the breath. If the breath stops, the pose is currently too much. Bend the knees more or come to hands and knees.
  • Staying too long, too early. Five clean breaths beats thirty seconds of shaking. Wrist and shoulder tolerance is built over weeks.
Accessible modifications

Each of these is a complete version of the pose, not a reduced one. Choose the one that lets you breathe evenly and keep a long spine.

At a wall

Place your hands flat on a wall at about hip height and walk your feet back until your torso is horizontal and your arms are straight. You get the same shoulder work and the same spinal length with none of the wrist load. This is the version to use if your wrists object, and it is often the better teacher of the shape.

Hands on a chair

Set a sturdy chair against a wall so it cannot slide. Put your hands on the seat, walk back and lower your chest toward the floor. This sits between the wall version and the full pose, and it keeps your head above your heart if that is preferable today.

Hands on blocks

Set two blocks at their tallest height, shoulder-width apart, and put a hand on each. Raising the floor reduces the angle at the wrist and makes it far easier to keep the spine long. Blocks with a flat, wide face work better than rounded ones here.

Forearms down

Come onto your forearms with the elbows under the shoulders and the hands shoulder-width apart, then lift the hips. This removes the wrists from the equation entirely and asks more of the shoulders. Placing a folded blanket under the forearms makes it kinder on the elbows.

Progression

Once you can hold eight even breaths with a long spine and quiet hands, add a slow walk: bend one knee and press the opposite heel gently toward the floor, then change sides, five or six times before settling into stillness. From there, the next step is Three-Legged Dog, lifting one leg behind you while keeping both hips level and both hands equally weighted. Level hips matter far more than height.

Contraindications and cautions

Approach with care or choose the wall version if you have wrist pain, a recent wrist or shoulder injury, carpal tunnel symptoms, uncontrolled high blood pressure, glaucoma or a recent eye procedure, a recent head or neck injury, or if you are in late pregnancy. Come out of the pose if you feel pinching in the shoulder or wrist joint, numbness or tingling in the hands, or pressure in the head.

Why this pose is worth the time

Downward-Facing Dog turns up so often in class that it functions less like a pose and more like a hallway. In a flowing sequence you might pass through it fifteen times in an hour. That repetition is exactly why the setup matters: a small habit repeated fifteen times per class, three times a week, is a habit you will have for years.

Physically, the pose asks the shoulder joint to move into flexion while bearing weight, lengthens the muscles along the back of the legs and the calves, and requires the spinal extensors to hold length against gravity. It also puts real load through the wrists, which is the reason so many people quietly dread it and the reason the wall version exists.

If you are building a practice from scratch, spend the first two weeks in the wall version and the block version before you spend any time in the full shape. Nothing is lost. The shoulders learn the same thing, and the wrists get the weeks they need.
