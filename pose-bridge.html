Bridge Pose

Setu Bandha Sarvāṅgāsana  (SAY-too BAHN-dah sar-vahn-GAH-suh-nuh)

Lying on your back with the feet planted and the hips lifted. Setu bandha means the forming of a bridge. It runs two ways: as active leg and hip work, or with a block under the pelvis as a supported shape held for several minutes.

Pose breakdown

Setu Bandha Sarvāṅgāsana

  • Beginner
  • Hips
  • Spine
  • Block version available
Difficulty
Beginner in both the active and supported versions.
Breath pattern
Active version: even breath, inhale 4, exhale 4. Supported version: inhale 4, exhale 6.
Suggested hold
Active: 5 to 8 breaths. Supported on a block: 2 to 5 minutes.
What moves
Hip extension, hamstring and glute engagement, spinal extension, front-of-shoulder length
Props that help
A block, a bolster, a folded blanket under the shoulders, a strap around the thighs
Where it appears
Evening Wind-Down, Gentle Full Practice
Entry steps
  • Lie on your back with your knees bent and your feet flat on the floor.
  • Walk your feet back until your fingertips can just graze your heels. This is the correct distance for your proportions.
  • Set your feet hip-width apart with the outer edges parallel to the edges of the mat.
  • Rest your arms alongside your body with the palms facing down.
  • Press evenly through both feet, from the base of the big toe to the outer heel.
  • Lift your hips away from the floor, leading with the pubic bone rather than the lower back.
  • Roll your shoulders slightly underneath you and let the chest lift toward the chin.
  • Keep the back of the neck long and your gaze straight up. Do not turn your head while the hips are lifted.
Alignment cues
  • Lift comes from the legs. The height is produced by pressing the feet down, not by squeezing the lower back.
  • Knees stay hip-width. They will want to splay outward. A block held lightly between the thighs solves this in one move.
  • Knees track forward over the toes, not inward and not out past the outer foot.
  • Chest toward chin, not chin toward chest. The opening happens at the top of the chest, and the neck stays long and uncompressed.
  • Feet fully weighted. If the toes are gripping or the inner edges are lifting, walk the feet slightly further from the hips.
  • Head still. Turning the head under load is the one thing to avoid outright in this pose.
Common mistakes
  • Feet too far from the hips. The pose becomes a hamstring cramp waiting to happen and the hips never really lift.
  • Knees splaying wide. The lift collapses into the lower back. Hold a block between the thighs.
  • Chasing height. How high the hips go is not the point and is limited by hip and spine proportions. Evenness matters more than altitude.
  • Pushing into the neck. If you feel pressure in the throat or the back of the neck, lower down and place a folded blanket under the shoulders so the neck keeps its natural curve.
  • Turning the head mid-pose. Common and worth breaking as a habit. Come down first, then move your head.
  • Skipping the supported version. It does something different from the active one and is worth its own time.
Accessible modifications

All four are complete versions. The supported one in particular is not a scaled-down variant; it is a different pose with a different purpose.

Supported on a block

Lift the hips, slide a block underneath the bony part of the pelvis at its lowest or medium height, then release your weight onto it. Nothing needs to work. Stay for two to five minutes with a long exhale. If a block feels hard, use a firm bolster or a stack of two folded blankets instead.

Blanket under the shoulders

Fold a firm blanket into a rectangle and place it under your shoulders so the head and neck rest lower than the shoulder tops. This preserves the natural curve at the back of the neck under load, and it is a standard setup rather than a special accommodation.

Strap around the thighs

Loop a strap around both thighs just above the knees and tighten it to hip-width. The strap gives your legs something to press outward against, which keeps the knees tracking correctly without you having to think about it for the whole hold.

Small lift only

Lift the hips one or two inches and hold there. This is the whole pose for many people, particularly anyone with a sensitive lower back. A small even lift held with a steady breath is worth more than a large uneven one.

Progression

Once eight breaths in the active version feel even, add a set of slow lifts and lowers: inhale to lift, exhale to lower one vertebra at a time, six to eight times. When that is comfortable, try lifting one foot a few inches off the floor while keeping both hips exactly level, which is considerably harder than it sounds. The next pose along this line is Wheel, a full backbend on the hands and feet, and that one genuinely belongs in a room with a teacher in it.

Contraindications and cautions

Approach with care or choose the small-lift or supported version if you have a neck injury or neck pain, a recent shoulder injury, a lower back injury, uncontrolled high blood pressure, or if you are in the second or third trimester of pregnancy, when lying flat on the back for extended periods is generally not advised. Do not turn your head while the hips are lifted. Come out of the pose if you feel pressure in the head or throat, sharp sensation in the lower back, or cramping in the hamstrings.

Two poses wearing one name

Active Bridge and supported Bridge share a shape and share nothing else. In the active version your legs are working, your glutes and hamstrings are engaged, and thirty seconds is a real thirty seconds. In the supported version a block carries your pelvis, your legs do nothing, and the useful duration is measured in minutes.

Sequences use them at different moments for that reason. The gentle full practice uses the active version late in the standing work, while it still wants your legs awake. The evening wind-down uses the supported version for a long unhurried hold, when the goal is stillness rather than effort.

If you only have time for one, the supported version is the one most people under-practice. Setting a block under your hips and doing nothing for four minutes is harder to justify to yourself than a set of repetitions, and it is often the more useful of the two.
