Find a teacher

A directory of yoga teachers and studios across the United States. Basic listings are free. Featured placement is paid and labeled as such. What we verify and what we do not is stated in full below.

Read before using this directory

Longhold Yoga does not vet, endorse, certify, employ or supervise anyone listed in this directory. Listings are submitted by the teachers and studios themselves and are published largely as written. Verify credentials, insurance and experience yourself before booking. How to do that is set out further down this page.

How the directory works

There are two tiers, and the difference between them is placement, not endorsement.

  • Basic listing. Free, permanently. Business name, city and state, class formats taught, whether the space is accessible, contact link and website. Listed alphabetically within each state. No payment is ever required to appear.
  • Featured placement. Paid. Appears above the basic listings for its metropolitan area, with a photograph and a longer description. Every featured listing carries a visible Featured placement label wherever it appears. Featured placement buys position and space. It does not buy an endorsement, a review, or a claim about quality.

Sorting within each tier is alphabetical. There is no ranking, no star rating and no score, because we have no way to measure teaching quality at a distance and would rather say so than invent a number.

How to check a teacher yourself

Since we cannot verify teaching quality from a distance, here is what to do instead. None of it is awkward to ask, and every reasonable teacher expects these questions.

  • Ask what training they completed, with whom, and over what period. A clear, specific answer is the signal. Vagueness about where and when is the thing to notice.
  • Ask whether they carry professional liability insurance. Most working teachers in the United States do, and the answer should be immediate.
  • Ask how long they have taught the specific format you want. Someone can be very experienced in Vinyasa and new to teaching Restorative, which is worth knowing.
  • Ask how they handle hands-on adjustments. A good answer describes a consent process. Many studios use consent cards. If you would rather not be touched, saying so is a complete sentence and requires no reason.
  • Ask what happens if you need to modify something. The answer tells you a lot about whether props are treated as standard equipment or as an exception.
  • Read the class description, not just the level. "Level one" at one studio can be brisker than "level two" at another.
  • Watch a class before booking a package. Many studios allow this. If you have a health condition, an injury or an access requirement, this is worth the trip.

On credentials generally: registration with a teaching organization records that a training was completed. It does not assess an individual teacher's skill, and it is not a healthcare qualification. A yoga teacher is not a physical therapist, a physician or a clinician, and should not present themselves as one.

If you need specific accommodations

Contact the studio before you arrive rather than on the day. Useful things to ask about: step-free entry and elevator access, whether chairs are available for seated practice, whether the room is quiet or plays music, whether the lights are dimmed, whether mats and props are provided or you should bring your own, and what the changing room arrangements are.

Studios that answer these questions readily and specifically are the ones worth going to.
