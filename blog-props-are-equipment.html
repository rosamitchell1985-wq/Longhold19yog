Props are equipment, not training wheels

Published 05/13/2026 Updated 08/27/2026 About 5 minutes to read Longhold Yoga editorial

In almost every beginner class there is a moment when the teacher offers blocks and a portion of the room declines. Not because those people have considered it and decided the block would not help. Because taking the block feels like an admission.

That instinct is worth examining, because it is based on a misunderstanding of what a prop does.

A block does not make a pose easier. It makes it a different pose.

Take Triangle Pose. You stand with your feet wide, straighten the front leg, and tip sideways over it, reaching the lower hand toward the floor.

For most people, the floor is not reachable with a long spine. So something has to give, and what gives is the spine: it rounds sideways, the chest turns down toward the mat, and the lower hand arrives at the shin or the ankle. The pose now looks approximately correct from a distance.

What is actually happening is that a pose intended to lengthen one side of the torso while the legs work has become a pose in which the torso collapses and the hamstrings complain. The two are not versions of the same thing. They are different activities that happen to share a silhouette.

Put a block under the lower hand and the geometry changes. The floor has arrived early. The spine can stay long, the chest can stay open toward the side of the room, and the legs can do the work they were meant to do. That is the pose. Without the block, you were doing something else.

This is the general case. A prop does not reduce a pose. It removes the reason the pose was deforming.

Three things props actually do

There are only three mechanisms, and every prop use is one of them.

  • They bring the floor closer. A block under a hand, under a hip, under a forehead. The ground arrives at a height your body can reach without distorting.
  • They make your arms longer. A strap around the feet in a seated forward fold. Your arms are now long enough that your spine does not have to round to compensate.
  • They hold your weight. A bolster under the torso in Child's Pose, a block under the pelvis in Bridge Pose. No muscle has to work, which is what makes a five-minute hold possible.

Notice that none of these is "makes it less demanding." The third one in particular makes a pose possible that is not otherwise possible at all. A supported Bridge held for four minutes is not an easier version of active Bridge. It is a thing you can only do with a block.

The people who use the most props have practiced the longest

Walk into an Iyengar studio, which is the lineage most associated with prop use, and the room will contain blocks, straps, bolsters, blankets, chairs and ropes anchored to the wall. The teacher, who may have twenty or thirty years of practice and an unusually long formal training behind them, will be using them too.

Much of the prop vocabulary the rest of yoga now uses came from that tradition, and it did not arrive as a concession to beginners. It arrived as a way of making specific pose actions accessible to specific bodies, precisely so that experienced practitioners could work on the thing the pose was for rather than on the thing their proportions made difficult.

Restorative practice makes the point even more directly. A restorative class is not a beginner class. It is a format built entirely on props, in which nothing happens without them, and it is practiced by people at every level.

The proportions argument

Here is the part that is worth sitting with.

How far you can fold forward in a seated forward fold is determined by several things you did not choose and cannot change: the length of your femurs relative to your torso, the depth and angle of your hip sockets, the shape of the top of your thigh bone, and how far your pelvis can tip before your spine has to take over.

Two people with identical hamstring length can be eight inches apart in that pose. Permanently. No amount of practice closes that gap, because the gap is skeletal.

Which means that "reaching your feet" is not a measure of anything about your practice. It is a measure of your proportions. A strap around the feet is not a substitute for the real version. It is what lets a person with a long femur do the same hip hinge that a person with a short femur can do without one.

The same logic applies to heels in Downward-Facing Dog, hands on the floor in a standing fold, and whether a block is needed in Triangle. All of these are widely treated as achievement markers. None of them is.

How this site treats props

Every pose guide here lists at least two prop-supported options, and they are written as equal alternatives rather than as fallbacks. The wall version of Downward-Facing Dog is not the beginner version; it is the version that gives you the shoulder action without the wrist load, and it is the right choice for a great many people permanently.

The props guide lists a household substitute for every item, because nothing on this site requires a purchase. A hardback book is a block. A bathrobe belt is a strap. A folded bath towel is a blanket. Two firm pillows in one pillowcase is a bolster.

And every prop tag in the pose library names the prop that opens a pose up, not a prop you are required to own. Triangle is tagged with a block because a block is what lets most people keep the spine long. Practicing it without one is still practicing it.

The practical version

Next class, take the block. Not because you need it and not because you do not. Take it, use it in one pose where you would normally reach without it, and notice whether the pose changes.

Most people find that it does, and that what changes is not how hard the pose is but where they feel it. That is the whole argument, and it is more convincing in the body than on a page.

Health and safety

This article is general movement education, not medical advice. Props change how a pose loads your body but they do not make any pose risk-free, and nothing described here treats or prevents any health condition. Speak with a qualified healthcare professional before beginning a new physical practice, particularly if you are pregnant, recently injured, post-surgical or managing a health condition.
