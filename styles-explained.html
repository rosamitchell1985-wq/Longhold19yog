Yoga styles explained

Class listings assume you already know what these words mean. Here is what actually happens in a Hatha, Vinyasa, Yin, Restorative, Iyengar or Ashtanga class: how fast it moves, how long poses are held, what props appear, and which one suits a first visit.

A useful thing to understand first: these labels describe format, not quality, and they overlap considerably. A class labeled Hatha at one studio may look almost identical to a class labeled slow flow at another. The labels tell you roughly how fast the room will move and roughly how long you will stay in each shape, and that is most of what you need before walking in.

What follows is arranged from the least to the most demanding on a first visit.

Restorative

Poses per hour
Six to eight
Typical hold
5 to 20 minutes
Props
Extensive. Bolsters, blankets, blocks, straps, eye covers
Pace
Almost entirely still
Good first class
Yes, for anyone
What to expect
Long silences, dim light, help arranging props

A restorative class holds a small number of shapes for a long time, with every part of the body supported by props so no muscle has to work. Setting up a single pose can take two or three minutes, and the teacher will often help you arrange bolsters and blankets. You will spend most of the hour lying down.

It is the gentlest format on this list by a wide margin, and it is the one most people are surprised by. Doing nothing for fifteen minutes in a supported shape is not the same as sitting on a sofa, and it asks a kind of patience that has nothing to do with fitness.

If you have never taken a class and want the least intimidating entry point, this is it. Bring warm layers and socks. Body temperature drops when you stop moving.

Hatha

Poses per hour
Fifteen to twenty-five
Typical hold
3 to 8 breaths
Props
Blocks and straps, used as needed
Pace
Moderate, with pauses between poses
Good first class
Yes, especially if labeled beginner or fundamentals
What to expect
Instruction, setup time, poses held rather than passed through

Hatha is the broadest label in modern studio scheduling. Strictly, the word covers most physical yoga practice, which is why it ends up on the schedule as a catch-all for a general-level class that holds poses for several breaths at a moderate pace.

In practice: you set a pose up, hold it for three to eight breaths while the teacher gives instructions, come out, and move on. There is time to hear a cue and apply it, which is the main advantage over faster formats. Most of the poses in the library appear regularly in Hatha classes.

For a first studio class, Hatha labeled beginner, fundamentals or level one is the standard recommendation and it is a good one.

Yin

Poses per hour
Twelve to eighteen, counting both sides
Typical hold
2 to 5 minutes
Props
Bolsters, blocks and blankets, used to support long holds
Pace
Very slow, mostly seated and lying
Good first class
Yes, but expect it to be mentally demanding
What to expect
Long stillness, muscles deliberately relaxed, quiet room

Yin holds mostly seated and lying shapes for two to five minutes each, with the muscles deliberately relaxed rather than engaged. The idea is that longer, gentler loading affects the denser connective tissues around the joints differently from short, muscular holding. Poses often have their own Yin names, so Pigeon may be called Sleeping Swan and Bound Angle may be called Butterfly.

Physically it is undemanding. Mentally it is not. Three minutes in a shape with nothing to do is where most people discover how much of their practice was staying busy. Fidgeting in the second minute is standard and worth expecting.

Two practical notes. Wear more layers than you think, because you will be still. And take the shape less deeply than feels available at the start, because three minutes changes what a position feels like considerably.

Iyengar

Poses per hour
Eight to fifteen
Typical hold
30 seconds to several minutes
Props
Central. Blocks, straps, chairs, bolsters, wall ropes, blankets
Pace
Deliberate, with long verbal instruction
Good first class
Yes, and the teaching is unusually precise
What to expect
Detailed alignment cues, repeated setups, teachers with long formal training

Iyengar is named for B. K. S. Iyengar and is defined by precision and by the use of props. You may spend eight minutes on a single standing pose, setting it up, coming out, adjusting one thing and setting it up again. Props are not accommodations here; they are standard equipment used by everyone in the room, and much of the prop vocabulary the rest of yoga now uses came from this lineage.

Teacher training in this system is notably long and formal compared to most others, and the instruction reflects that. If you like knowing exactly what your back foot should be doing and why, this is the format that will tell you.

It is an excellent first class for anyone who wants to understand alignment, and it is the format most likely to give you a specific, useful answer to a specific question about your own body.

Vinyasa

Poses per hour
Forty or more
Typical hold
1 to 5 breaths
Props
Blocks, occasionally. Less prop use than other formats
Pace
Continuous, timed to the breath
Good first class
Only if labeled slow flow, beginner flow or level one
What to expect
Movement almost the whole hour, repeated sequences, less individual instruction

Vinyasa links poses into continuous sequences timed to the breath, so one movement happens per inhale or exhale. The class moves nearly the whole time and often repeats a sequence several times with variations. Music is common. It is currently the most widely taught format in the United States.

The pace is the thing to be aware of. In a fast Vinyasa class you may pass through Downward-Facing Dog fifteen times without anyone having time to tell you what your shoulders should be doing. That is not a criticism of the format, but it does mean a first-timer can spend an hour repeating an unhelpful habit.

If Vinyasa is what your local studio offers, look specifically for slow flow, beginner flow or level one. Avoid anything labeled level two, power, or advanced for a first visit. Coming to rest in Child's Pose whenever you want to is completely normal, and every teacher will say so at the start.

Ashtanga

Poses per hour
Fixed sequence, roughly seventy in the primary series
Typical hold
5 breaths
Props
Rare
Pace
Vigorous and continuous
Good first class
Only in a led beginner class or a supervised Mysore room
What to expect
The same sequence every time, early mornings, a strong physical demand

Ashtanga practices a fixed sequence of poses in the same order every time. There are several series; nearly everyone works within the primary series for years. Two formats exist: a led class where a teacher counts the whole room through, and a Mysore-style room where everyone practices their own portion of the sequence at their own pace while a teacher moves around giving individual attention.

It is physically demanding and it is repetitive by design. The repetition is the method: practicing the same sequence six days a week means you are measuring changes against a fixed reference rather than against a different class every time.

The Mysore room is, counterintuitively, often the better place for a beginner than a led class, because a teacher will start you with the first handful of poses and nothing more. A led primary series class as a first-ever yoga class is not a good idea.

Which one to try first

If you want the shortest possible answer: look for a class labeled beginner, fundamentals or level one, in any of Hatha, Iyengar, Yin or Restorative. Any of those four will give you time to hear an instruction and apply it, which is the thing a first class is actually for.

If your only local option is Vinyasa, take the slowest one on the schedule, stand near the back, and rest whenever you want to.

Health and safety

These descriptions are general education about class formats, not medical advice, and no style of yoga treats or prevents any health condition. Speak with a qualified healthcare professional before beginning a new physical practice, particularly if you are pregnant, recently injured, post-surgical or managing a health condition.
