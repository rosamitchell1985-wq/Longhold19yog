Tree Pose

Vṛkṣāsana  (vrik-SHAH-suh-nuh)

Standing on one leg with the other foot pressed into the ankle, calf or inner thigh. Vṛkṣa means tree. It is the pose people most often describe as the one they cannot do, usually because they are trying to do it in the middle of the room instead of beside a wall.

Pose breakdown

Vṛkṣāsana

  • Beginner
  • Balance
  • Legs
  • Wall recommended
Difficulty
Beginner at a wall. Intermediate free-standing.
Breath pattern
Even breath. Inhale 4 counts, exhale 4 counts. Balance follows the breath.
Suggested hold
5 to 8 breaths per side, roughly 30 to 45 seconds
What moves
Ankle and foot stabilizers, hip abductors on the standing leg, hip external rotation on the lifted leg
Props that help
A wall, a chair back, a block between the foot and the leg
Where it appears
10-Minute Morning, Gentle Full Practice
Entry steps
  • Stand with your feet hip-width apart and spread all ten toes.
  • Shift your weight into your left foot without letting your left hip swing out to the side.
  • Turn your right knee out to the side and bend it.
  • Place the sole of your right foot on your left ankle, calf or inner thigh. Never on the side of the knee joint.
  • Press the foot into the leg and the leg back into the foot with equal pressure, so the two hold each other.
  • Draw your right hip down so both hip points stay level and continue facing forward.
  • Bring your hands together in front of your chest, or reach both arms overhead if your balance is already steady.
  • Fix your eyes on a point that does not move, at about eye height, a few feet in front of you.
Alignment cues
  • Never the knee. The lifted foot goes above or below the standing knee, never on the side of it. This is the one non-negotiable rule of the pose.
  • Mutual pressure. Foot presses leg, leg presses foot. Without that, the foot slides and you spend the hold fighting gravity instead of standing.
  • Hips level. The standing hip wants to push out sideways. Draw the lifted hip down and the standing hip in until both points are level.
  • Standing leg awake, knee soft. Engage the thigh but do not lock the knee back. A locked knee is brittle rather than stable.
  • Foot tripod. Weight distributed between the base of the big toe, the base of the little toe and the center of the heel.
  • Steady gaze. Look at something stationary. Balance degrades noticeably when the eyes wander.
Common mistakes
  • Foot on the knee. Puts sideways pressure on a joint that does not move sideways. Move it to the ankle or the thigh.
  • Hip jutting out. Creates a curve through the whole spine and makes balance harder, not easier. Level the hips and the pose steadies.
  • Holding the breath. Extremely common in balance poses, and it actively makes balancing harder. If the breath stops, lower the foot.
  • Treating a wobble as failure. Wobbling is the ankle stabilizers doing their job. It is the work, not a sign you are failing at it.
  • Starting in the middle of the room. There is no reason to. Stand next to a wall for the first few weeks and you will spend the time practicing rather than catching yourself.
  • Arms overhead too soon. Raising the arms moves your center of mass upward. Hands at the chest until the legs are reliable.
Accessible modifications

Each of these is a complete version of the pose. None of them is a lesser one.

Fingertips on a wall

Stand with the wall a forearm's length to your side and rest two or three fingertips against it. Just enough contact to give your nervous system a reference point. This removes the fear of falling, which is often the thing actually making the pose hard, and it lets you spend the hold working on level hips.

Toes on the floor, heel on the ankle

Rest the toes of your lifted foot on the floor beside the standing foot and place only the heel against the standing ankle. This is a kickstand: you get the hip rotation and the weight shift with a second point of contact on the ground. It is a permanent option for anyone with ankle instability.

Holding a chair back

Stand behind a sturdy chair and hold the back with one or both hands. Useful if you are working with any dizziness, if the floor is uneven, or if you simply want to hold for two full minutes and study what the standing hip is doing.

Seated version

Sit tall on a firm chair with both feet flat. Cross your right ankle over your left thigh, let the right knee fall out to the side, and press the right knee gently away while sitting up tall. You get the hip rotation and the mutual pressure with no balance demand at all.

Progression

Once you can hold eight breaths at the wall on both sides without touching it, move a step away from the wall and repeat, keeping the hands at the chest. When that is steady, add the arms overhead. After that, the useful progression is not a harder pose but a longer one: work toward two minutes per side. If you want a further challenge, close one eye, then both, which removes the visual reference and hands the entire job to the ankle and hip.

Contraindications and cautions

Use the wall, the chair or the seated version if you experience dizziness or vertigo, have low blood pressure, are recovering from an inner ear condition, have ankle or knee instability, a recent hip or knee replacement, or if you are pregnant, when the center of gravity shifts week to week. Practice on a firm level surface rather than a thick carpet or an uneven floor. Come out of the pose if you feel sharp sensation in the standing knee or the outer hip.

Balance is a skill, not a trait

People arrive at Tree Pose with a settled belief about whether they have good balance. Almost always that belief was formed years ago and has not been tested since.

Standing on one leg is a task your nervous system performs by making constant small corrections based on information from the sole of your foot, from your inner ear and from your eyes. All three of those improve with practice, and they improve fairly quickly. Two weeks of thirty seconds per side, most days, produces a noticeable difference in how much correcting your ankle has to do.

What does not help is treating each wobble as evidence. The wobble is the training. A person standing perfectly still in Tree Pose is not balancing better than a person wobbling; they are just further along the same process.

It also varies day to day, considerably. Tiredness, a long drive, a new pair of shoes and the surface you are standing on all change it. A wobbly Tuesday after a steady Monday means nothing.
