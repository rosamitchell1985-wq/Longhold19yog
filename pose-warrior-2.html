Warrior II

Vīrabhadrāsana II  (veer-ah-bah-DRAH-suh-nuh)

A wide stance with the front knee bent and both arms reaching in opposite directions at shoulder height. It is named for Vīrabhadra, a figure from an old story, and it is usually the first pose that teaches people what standing work actually asks of the legs.

Pose breakdown

Vīrabhadrāsana II

  • Beginner
  • Hips
  • Legs
  • Chair version available
Difficulty
Beginner. The demand is on endurance rather than coordination.
Breath pattern
Even breath. Inhale 4 counts, exhale 4 counts, through the nose.
Suggested hold
5 to 8 breaths per side, roughly 30 to 45 seconds
What moves
Hip external rotation on the front leg, quadriceps and glute work, ankle stability, shoulder endurance
Props that help
A chair, a wall, a block under the front thigh
Where it appears
Gentle Full Practice
Entry steps
  • Stand tall with your feet together and your arms at your sides.
  • Step your feet wide apart. Three to four feet is typical; taller bodies need more. A rough guide is that your ankles sit roughly under your wrists when the arms are out.
  • Turn your right foot out ninety degrees so the toes point to the short edge of the mat.
  • Angle your left foot in about fifteen degrees. The back foot stays fully grounded, especially the outer edge.
  • Line up your right heel with the arch of your left foot.
  • Bend your right knee toward a right angle, tracking the kneecap over the second and third toes.
  • Reach your arms out to shoulder height, palms facing down, and let your shoulders drop away from your ears.
  • Turn your head to look past your right hand, keeping your neck long.
Alignment cues
  • Knee over ankle, not past it. If the knee travels beyond the ankle, widen the stance rather than bending less deeply.
  • Knee tracking outward. The front knee wants to fall inward. Press the outer edge of the front foot down and think about drawing the knee gently toward the little-toe side.
  • Torso stacked over the pelvis. The upper body stays upright and centered between the legs. Leaning toward the front leg is the most common drift.
  • Back leg working. The back leg is straight and active, pressing the outer heel down. It is not a kickstand.
  • Arms at shoulder height, not above. Reach out through the fingertips in both directions rather than lifting the shoulders.
  • Hips facing the long edge. Both hip points aim toward the side of the mat, not the front.
Common mistakes
  • Stance too narrow. The most frequent cause of a knee traveling past the ankle. Step wider before you bend deeper.
  • Back heel lifting. The back foot loses contact and the whole pose floats. Shorten the stance until the outer heel stays down.
  • Leaning forward over the front thigh. Feels like effort, is actually a collapse. Stack the ribs over the pelvis.
  • Shoulders creeping toward the ears. Reach wide instead of up, and lower the arms if they will not stay level for the whole hold.
  • Front knee collapsing inward. This is a strength and awareness issue, not a flexibility one. Press the outer front foot down and go less deep.
  • Holding the breath during the last few seconds. A reliable sign to shorten the hold to three or four breaths and build up.
Accessible modifications

Each of these is a complete version of the pose. Pick the one that lets you hold a steady breath.

Seated in a chair

Sit sideways on a sturdy chair with your right thigh along the seat and your right knee bent over the front edge. Extend your left leg out to the side with the foot turned in, then take the arms wide. You get the same hip rotation, the same arm work and the same breath, with the chair carrying your weight. This is the version used in the Desk Reset sequence.

Back against a wall

Stand with your back and both heels lightly touching a wall and set the pose up from there. The wall tells you immediately if your torso is drifting forward or your hips are turning, and it takes the balance question away so you can pay attention to the legs.

Shallower knee bend

Bend the front knee only to forty-five degrees instead of ninety. This is a legitimate and permanent option, not a stepping stone. Anyone with knee sensitivity, and anyone new to standing work, should start here and stay as long as it is useful.

Arms lowered

Rest your hands on your hips or let the arms hang. Holding the arms at shoulder height is a separate piece of endurance work from the legs, and there is no reason to let it be the thing that ends the pose early.

Progression

When eight breaths per side feel steady with a ninety-degree front knee and a quiet breath, lengthen the hold to twelve breaths rather than deepening the shape. After that, Warrior II becomes the entry point for Extended Side Angle, where the front forearm rests on the thigh and the top arm reaches long overhead, and for Triangle Pose, where the front leg straightens and the torso tips sideways over it with the lower hand on a block.

Contraindications and cautions

Approach with care, use the chair version, or reduce the knee bend if you have knee pain, a recent knee or hip injury or replacement, ankle instability, sacroiliac discomfort, a shoulder injury that makes holding the arms out painful, or if you are in later pregnancy when a wide stance may feel unstable. Come out of the pose if you feel pinching in the front hip, sharp sensation on the inside of the knee, or if your breath cannot stay even.

The part nobody mentions

Warrior II is usually the first pose in which a beginner discovers that a pose can be difficult without being complicated. There is nothing to figure out. The shape is obvious from across the room. And forty-five seconds in, the front thigh is burning and the arms want to come down.

That is the pose working, and it is also the reason the shallow-knee version deserves more respect than it gets. A forty-five degree bend held with an even breath and a stacked torso teaches your legs more than a ninety-degree bend held with your ribs collapsed toward the front thigh and your breath stopped.

The second thing worth knowing: your back foot is doing more than it appears to. If you lift the back heel even slightly, the pose loses its base and the front knee immediately takes more than its share. Shortening the stance until that outer heel stays reliably down is one of the highest-value adjustments available in standing work.
