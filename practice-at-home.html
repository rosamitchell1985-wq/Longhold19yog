Practice at home

How much floor you actually need, how to check your surface, what to do about carpet, noise, roommates and small apartments, and how to build a routine that survives a bad week.

How much space you need

Less than you think, and the number is specific. Lie on the floor and reach your arms overhead. The rectangle you occupy plus about a foot in every direction is the whole requirement. For most adults that is roughly 7 feet by 4 feet, or about 28 square feet.

Two additional things are worth having, and both are free:

  • Three feet of clear wall, with four feet of floor in front of it. Check for baseboards, radiators, light switches and picture hooks at shoulder height.
  • Enough ceiling height to reach both arms overhead while standing. Obvious until you set up under a sloped attic ceiling and discover Mountain Pose does not fit.

You do not need a dedicated room, and for most people a dedicated room is a way of making the practice conditional on having one. A cleared patch of living room floor works. So does the space at the foot of a bed, or a hallway with the rug rolled back.

Checking your surface

The floor matters more than the room. Three things to check before your first practice.

  • Is it level? Put a mat down and stand on one foot near each end. An uneven floor makes balance work confusing rather than difficult, because you cannot tell what is you and what is the floor.
  • Does the mat slide? On polished hardwood or tile, a mat can travel during a lunge. Put a thin rug or a folded blanket underneath it. On laminate, check whether the mat slides when you press your hands into it in a plank position.
  • Is it hard enough? A very thick carpet or a rug with deep pile makes standing poses unstable and makes balance work considerably harder. A firm surface under the mat is better than a soft one.

If you only have carpet

Low-pile carpet is fine and needs nothing. Medium pile is workable with a mat on top. Deep pile or a thick rug pad is the one genuinely difficult surface, and there are two straightforward answers: put a hard board or a large cutting board under the mat where your standing foot goes, or move the practice to a kitchen or bathroom floor and take a folded blanket for your knees.

If you have no floor space at all

Practice from a chair. This is a real option, not a consolation. The Desk Reset sequence uses nothing but a chair and a desk, and every pose guide on this site includes a chair or wall version. A studio apartment with a chair has everything required.

Setting up in four steps

  1. Pick the spot and leave the mat there

    The strongest predictor of whether a home practice continues is not motivation, it is friction. A mat that lives rolled up in a closet adds a decision every single time. A mat already unrolled removes it. If you cannot leave it out, at least leave it rolled somewhere you pass daily rather than somewhere you have to go and get it.

  2. Put the props at the edge of the mat

    Two blocks, a strap and a folded blanket, stacked at one corner. In a twenty-minute sequence with long holds, getting up to fetch a block is the most common reason a practice ends early. A small basket by the wall keeps it tidy enough that nobody in the household complains.

  3. Deal with the phone before you start

    If you are using the timer on a sequence page, the phone or tablet needs to be there. Put it in do-not-disturb, face down, at the top of the mat where you can see the timer but not the notifications. If you are using a printed sequence, put the phone in another room entirely.

  4. Print the sequence and leave it out

    Every sequence page on this site prints as a clean practice handout, two or three sheets of letter paper, with the header, navigation, timer controls, ad slot, trailing commentary and footer all stripped out. The type is sized to be readable from standing. Print the one you plan to do most often and leave it beside the mat.

Other people, small apartments and noise

Practicing with people in the house

The most common obstacle to a home practice is not space, it is self-consciousness. Some things that help: practice at a time when the house is quietest rather than a time when it is empty, since waiting for empty means waiting. Tell the household what you are doing rather than trying to be discreet about it, because discretion is more distracting than the activity. And accept that someone will walk through. Nothing about this practice requires a closed door.

If you live below other people, or above them

Almost nothing in these sequences makes noise. There are no jumps and no impact anywhere in the four sequences on this site. The only sound is a mat unrolling. If you are worried, the evening wind-down is entirely on the floor and entirely still.

Working in a small apartment

Use the wall you have rather than the room you want. Practice along the longest clear line in the apartment, which is often a hallway. Roll the mat out perpendicular to a bed and use the bed frame as a chair substitute. If you have a coffee table, it may be exactly the right height for a supported forward fold.

Pets

A cat will sit on the mat. This is universal and there is no solution. Give it a folded blanket in the corner of the mat and it will usually take that instead. A dog will interpret Downward-Facing Dog as an invitation, which is worth knowing before your first attempt.

Building a routine that survives a bad week

Every home practice eventually meets a week where it does not happen. Whether the practice continues depends almost entirely on what you decided in advance about that week.

Three things that make a difference:

  • Anchor the practice to something that already happens rather than to a time. "After I put the coffee on" survives a schedule change. "7:15 a.m." does not.
  • Have a version that takes three minutes. Decide now what your minimum is. Two minutes of Cat and Cow and one minute of Child's Pose is a legitimate practice, and it keeps the sequence unbroken on days that fall apart.
  • Do not restart, resume. Missing four days is not a failure that requires a fresh beginning. It is four days. The next practice is just the next practice.

A workable weekly shape for a first few months, which assumes nothing about your schedule beyond having some:

  • Three ten-minute mornings, whichever three are easiest.
  • One or two desk resets in the middle of a working day.
  • One evening wind-down.
  • One gentle full practice on the day you are least rushed, once that feels appealing rather than daunting.

That is roughly ninety minutes a week, in pieces small enough to survive most weeks.

Health and safety

Practicing alone means nobody is watching your alignment, so read the pose guides carefully and stop when something does not feel right.
