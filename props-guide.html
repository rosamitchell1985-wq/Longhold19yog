Props and modifications

What each prop actually does, how to size it, what you already own that will substitute, and how to modify common poses with a chair, a wall, a block, a strap or a blanket.

A prop does one of three things: it brings the floor closer to you, it makes your arms longer, or it holds the weight of some part of your body so no muscle has to. That is the whole theory. Everything below is an application of one of those three.

The framing that matters most: a prop changes what a pose does, it does not make a pose easier. A block under the hand in Triangle Pose lets your spine stay long, so the pose becomes a side-body pose instead of a hamstring negotiation. That is a different pose, not a reduced one. More on this idea here.

The mat

What it does
Gives grip, a defined working area and consistent padding under the knees
Standard size
68 by 24 inches. Long sizes run 72 to 84 inches
Thickness
3 to 5 mm for standing work. 6 mm or more for joint comfort
Household substitute
A folded blanket over a rug, or a bare low-pile carpet

If you are taller than about 5 feet 10 inches, get a 72-inch or longer mat so your head and heels both stay on it when you lie down. It is the single most common sizing regret.

Thickness is a genuine trade-off, not a quality tier. A thin mat gives you a stable base for standing poses and lets you feel the floor. A thick mat is kinder to knees and hips but makes balance work noticeably harder, because the surface moves under your foot. Many people who practice a lot own a thin mat and put a folded blanket under their knees when they need it.

Grip matters more than material marketing. If your hands slide forward in Downward-Facing Dog, that is a grip problem and no amount of technique will fix it.

Blocks

What it does
Brings the floor up to your hand, hip or head
Standard size
9 by 6 by 4 inches, giving three usable heights
How many
Two. Having two matters more than what they cost
Household substitute
A thick hardback book, a shoebox packed tight, a stack of magazines taped together

Cork blocks are heavier, denser and do not skid. Foam blocks are lighter, quieter to drop, and slightly kinder under a bony hip. Either works. Cork is the better choice if you will stand on the block or put much weight through it; foam is the better choice for supported and restorative work.

The three heights are the point. A block laid flat is 4 inches, on its side is 6 inches, and standing tall is 9 inches. Poses often want different heights on different days and on different sides.

Where a block goes:

  • Under the lower hand in Triangle Pose or Half Moon, so the spine stays long.
  • Under both hands in Standing Forward Fold or Low Lunge, so the arms reach the ground.
  • Under the forehead in Child's Pose, so the head is supported rather than hanging.
  • Under the pelvis in supported Bridge Pose, so the hips can rest for several minutes.
  • Between the thighs in Bridge Pose, so the knees stay hip-width without you thinking about it.
  • Under the sitting bones in Hero Pose, so the ankles and knees are not asked to fold all the way.

Straps

What it does
Makes your arms longer so your spine does not have to round to reach
Standard length
6 feet suits most people. 8 or 10 feet for taller bodies or behind-the-back work
Buckle
D-ring or cinch. Both work. D-ring holds tension better
Household substitute
A bathrobe belt, a long scarf, a luggage strap, a dog leash

The strap is the most under-used prop in home practice and the one that changes the most poses. Any time you are reaching for a foot and your back is rounding to get there, a strap solves it immediately.

Where a strap goes:

  • Around the balls of both feet in Seated Forward Fold, held with soft elbows.
  • Around one foot in Head-to-Knee Forward Bend or reclined leg stretches.
  • Around the back thigh in reclined figure four, so you are not holding on with your hands for two minutes.
  • Looped around both thighs above the knees in Bridge Pose, giving the legs something to press against.
  • Held between both hands for shoulder work, at whatever width lets the arms stay straight.

Blankets

What it does
Raises the hips, pads a joint, supports the head, keeps you warm
What to look for
Firm and densely woven. A fluffy blanket compresses to nothing
How many
Two is a good number for home practice
Household substitute
A folded bath towel, a firm woven throw, a folded flat bedsheet

The classic Mexican-style yoga blanket exists because it is firm enough to hold a shape when folded. Anything with the same density works. A duvet or a fleece throw does not, because it flattens under weight and you end up sitting on the floor anyway.

The most valuable use is the least glamorous: sitting on the front edge of a folded blanket in any seated pose so your hips are higher than your knees. For a large proportion of adults, this is the difference between a seated forward fold that works from the hips and one that works from the lower back.

Other uses: rolled under the ankles in Child's Pose and Hero Pose, rolled behind the knees to reduce knee flexion, under the shoulders in Bridge Pose to protect the neck curve, under the knees in final rest, and over you during a long rest when body temperature drops.

Bolsters

What it does
Holds the weight of the torso so no muscle has to work
Shapes
Round is firmer and higher. Rectangular is flatter and more stable
Typical size
About 25 inches long, 6 to 9 inches deep
Household substitute
Two firm bed pillows inside one pillowcase, or a tightly rolled duvet

A bolster is what makes a long hold possible. In restorative and Yin work, the difference between three comfortable minutes and ninety uncomfortable seconds is usually a bolster in the right place.

Rectangular bolsters are more useful for a first purchase: they are stable, they stack with blankets, and they sit flat under a torso. Round bolsters give more height and are better for opening across the chest.

Two firm pillows in a single pillowcase genuinely work. The pillowcase is not optional, because it stops the pillows sliding apart when you lie on them.

The two free props

The wall

A clear stretch of wall is the most useful prop most people already own, and it costs nothing. It gives feedback that no verbal cue can. In Warrior II, standing with your back against a wall tells you instantly whether your torso is drifting forward. In Tree Pose, two fingertips on the wall remove the fear of falling, which is often the thing actually making balance difficult. In Downward-Facing Dog, hands on the wall at hip height gives the whole shoulder action with no wrist load.

You need about three feet of clear wall for most uses and about four feet of floor in front of it. Check for baseboards and radiators before you commit.

The chair

A sturdy chair without wheels turns almost any pose into a version you can do. It supports the hands in a forward fold, holds a lifted foot in a lunge, becomes the seat for a seated Warrior II, and carries the calves in place of a wall for legs-up.

Requirements: a flat seat, no castors, no swivel, and no arms if you want to fold forward between them. A dining chair is ideal. Folding chairs work if they lock properly.

The entire Desk Reset sequence is built around a chair, and every pose guide on this site includes a chair or wall option written as an equal alternative.

What to buy first, in order

If you are starting from nothing and want to spread the cost: mat, then two blocks, then a strap, then a blanket, then a bolster. That order reflects how many poses each one changes, not how much it costs.

It is also entirely reasonable to buy none of it. A sturdy chair, a clear wall, a folded bath towel, a hardback book and a bathrobe belt cover every modification described on this page. Nothing on this site requires a purchase.

Health and safety

Props change how a pose loads your body but they do not make any pose risk-free, and nothing on this page treats or prevents any health condition. Speak with a qualified healthcare professional before beginning a new physical practice, particularly if you are pregnant, recently injured, post-surgical or managing a health condition. Stop if you feel pain, and work with a qualified teacher for hands-on guidance. Full disclaimer.
