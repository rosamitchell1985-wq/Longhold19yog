Start here: the complete beginner's guide

Everything you need before your first practice, including the questions people usually feel too self-conscious to ask out loud. Reading time is about twelve minutes.

What you actually need

Nothing, for the first few weeks. A firm floor, enough space to lie down with your arms out, and a sturdy chair will carry you through a month of practice. Carpet works. A rug over hardwood works. A cleared patch of a bedroom floor works.

If you decide to buy one thing, buy a mat. A mat gives you a defined edge to work inside, keeps your hands from sliding on a wood floor and puts a consistent surface under your knees. A standard mat is 68 inches long and 24 inches wide; if you are taller than about 5 feet 10 inches, look for a 72-inch or 84-inch length so your head and heels stay on it in a lying-down pose.

After the mat, in order of usefulness:

  • Two blocks. A block is a floor that arrives earlier. It goes under a hand in a standing pose, under the hips in a seated pose, or under the forehead in a forward fold. Cork blocks are heavier and steadier. Foam blocks are lighter and quieter. Standard size is 9 by 6 by 4 inches, and having two matters more than having expensive ones.
  • One strap. A strap is a longer arm. It loops around a foot so you can hold a leg without hauling your spine forward to reach. A 6-foot strap suits most people; 8-foot suits taller bodies and any pose where the strap goes behind the back. A bathrobe belt or a luggage strap does the same job.
  • One or two firm blankets. Folded, a blanket raises the hips in seated poses, cushions the knees, and covers you during the final rest, when body temperature drops.
  • A bolster, if you are drawn to slow, supported practice. A bolster is a firm cylinder or rectangle that holds the weight of the torso. Two firm bed pillows inside a pillowcase substitute well.

What to wear

Clothing you can bend, kneel and lie down in, that will not slide up over your head when you fold forward. That is the entire specification. Fitted at the ankle and the waist is more practical than fitted everywhere.

Practice barefoot if you can. Feet grip better than socks, and your toes are doing real work in standing poses. If bare feet are not comfortable or not possible, socks with rubber grip dots on the sole are the usual answer, and they are widely available.

Take off anything that will press into you when you lie on it. Belts, watches, hard hair clips and long necklaces all announce themselves once you are on your back.

How long and how often

Ten to twenty minutes, three or four days a week, is a workable first month for most adults. Frequency matters more than duration at the start. Four ten-minute practices teach your body more about where it is in space than one forty-minute practice on a Sunday, and they are far easier to keep doing.

A practical structure for the first month:

  • Two short mornings. The ten-minute morning sequence is designed for this and needs no props beyond a wall.
  • One or two workday breaks. The eight-minute desk reset runs from a chair in ordinary clothes.
  • One longer evening. The twenty-minute evening wind-down is entirely on the floor with long supported holds.

When those feel routine rather than novel, the forty-minute gentle full practice is the natural next step.

Why poses have two names

Nearly every pose here carries an English name and a Sanskrit name. Sanskrit is the classical language the older texts of yoga were written in, and the names are descriptive rather than decorative. Āsana means seat or pose, so the suffix appears on almost every one. Adho mukha means downward-facing. Śvāna means dog. Adho Mukha Śvānāsana is therefore, quite literally, downward-facing dog pose.

You do not need to learn Sanskrit to practice. You will hear the names in studio classes, so it helps to recognize a few, and knowing that eka pāda means one-foot or one-leg lets you decode a dozen pose names at once. The glossary lists sixty terms with a plain pronunciation guide.

Diacritics matter for accuracy. The line over the ā in āsana marks a long vowel, held roughly twice as long as a short one. We write them because leaving them out changes the word, in the same way that dropping an accent changes a French word.

What difficulty labels mean here

Difficulty labels on this site describe how much coordination and prior familiarity a pose asks for, not how worthy or advanced you are.

  • Beginner. Can be set up from a written description on a first attempt. Balance demands are low or a wall is available. Every one has a chair or floor option.
  • Intermediate. Asks you to coordinate two or three things at once, or to hold a position while breathing evenly. Usually easier once you have practiced the beginner poses it is built from.
  • Advanced. Requires range of motion, strength or balance that most people build over months or years, and is best learned with a teacher watching. We label these but do not push anyone toward them.

A beginner pose is not a lesser pose. Child's Pose is labeled beginner and is practiced by people who have been on a mat for thirty years.

Breathing while you move

The default instruction is simple: breathe in and out through your nose, evenly, without holding your breath. If you are holding your breath, the pose is asking for more than you have today, and the answer is to make the pose smaller.

Two patterns cover most of what you will meet:

  • Even breath. Inhale for four counts, exhale for four counts. Used while holding a standing pose.
  • Extended exhale. Inhale for four counts, exhale for six or eight. Used in supported and lying-down poses, and in the final rest.

The slim bar under the site header on every page counts one of these patterns for you. Press Start pacing and the bar fills as you breathe in and empties as you breathe out. On sequence pages it carries that sequence's own pattern. Breathwork Basics covers five patterns in detail, including when to stop.

Sensation, effort and pain

Learning to tell these apart is the most useful skill in a first year of practice.

  • Sensation is broad, spread over the belly of a muscle, and it eases as you keep breathing. It usually fades within a breath or two of coming out.
  • Effort is muscular work. Your leg shakes in a lunge. Your breath wants to get shorter. This is normal and it is a signal to hold for less time, not to grit your teeth.
  • Pain is sharp, hot, electrical, pinching, or located in a joint rather than a muscle. It does not ease as you breathe. It is a stop signal, not a challenge.

Come out of anything that hurts. That is not caution or a beginner's allowance; it is what experienced practitioners do, and it is the only reliable way to keep practicing for decades.

Your first four weeks

A schedule that has worked for a lot of people, and which assumes nothing about your starting range of motion.

  1. Week one: get on the floor

    Three sessions of the ten-minute morning sequence. Do not add anything. The only goal is that the mat stops feeling like an event. Expect it to feel awkward and slightly silly. That is not a sign of anything.

  2. Week two: add the desk reset

    Keep the three mornings and add two desk resets during the working day. This is where the practice starts fitting into your calendar rather than competing with it.

  3. Week three: read one pose properly

    Pick a pose you keep meeting, open its full guide, and read the alignment cues and the common mistakes before you practice it. Most people find at least one thing they had been doing without noticing.

  4. Week four: add one long practice

    Keep everything from week three and add either the evening wind-down or the gentle full practice on a day you are not rushed. Then repeat the whole month. Repetition is the point.

Going to a studio class

At some point most people want a teacher in the room, and there are things a website cannot do. A teacher sees the thing you cannot see, which is your own body from the outside.

Practical notes for a first class:

  • Arrive ten minutes early. Tell the teacher it is your first class. Every teacher wants to know this and none of them mind.
  • Mention anything relevant, briefly. Recent surgery, a pregnancy, a knee that objects to kneeling. You do not owe anyone a medical history, only the information that changes what they should offer you.
  • Look for a class labeled beginner, fundamentals, gentle, level one or all levels. Avoid anything labeled level two, power, or led primary series for a first visit.
  • You are allowed to rest. Sitting out a pose in Child's Pose is a normal thing that happens in every class, including among people who have practiced for years.
  • Ask about hands-on adjustments before class starts. Many studios use consent cards. If yours does not, saying "please no hands-on today" is a complete sentence.
